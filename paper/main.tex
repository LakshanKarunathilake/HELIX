\begin{abstract}
Linking entity mentions to canonical knowledge-base entries is central to
building structured biomedical resources, yet automated systems remain below the
precision that downstream use requires, leaving expert curation the bottleneck.
We present \emph{HELIX}, an open-source, configurable annotation tool that unites
hybrid candidate retrieval, LLM-based disambiguation, and knowledge-graph-aware
human review in a single expert-in-the-loop workflow over any FAISS-indexable
knowledge base. Retrieval fuses dense embeddings with a lexical index (BM25 and
character $n$-grams), directly targeting the failure mode in which dense encoders
produce uninformative embeddings for short mentions and abbreviations
(e.g.\ ``DM'' $\rightarrow$ \emph{Diabetes Mellitus}) that lexical matching
recovers. Over this grounded candidate set a large language model re-ranks
(retrieval-augmented), returning a best match with a rationale and confidence
while constrained to the retrieved identifiers. HELIX's distinguishing
contribution is a two-level, graph-aware annotation process: at the
\emph{individual} level, each mention's candidates are shown with their
neighbourhood in a connected knowledge graph (Neo4j), so annotators disambiguate
by graph context rather than surface text; at the \emph{document} level, the
accepted entities are assembled into one subgraph, isolated picks are flagged for
renegotiation, and identifiers can be rearranged until the document's entity set
is globally coherent. We evaluate HELIX on a biomedical entity-linking corpus,
reporting top-1 accuracy across lexical, dense, hybrid, and hybrid+LLM
configurations with breakdowns by mention length and entity type, and a
single-annotator case study of graph-assisted review. HELIX and a reproducible
setup are released to support scalable entity-linking annotation.

\keywords{Biomedical Entity Linking \and Knowledge Graphs \and
Retrieval-Augmented Generation \and Human-in-the-Loop Annotation \and
Hybrid Retrieval \and Collective Disambiguation.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Entity linking (EL) maps textual mentions to canonical entries in a knowledge
base (KB) such as the UMLS Metathesaurus~\cite{umls}, and is a prerequisite for
constructing biomedical knowledge graphs, normalising clinical text, and
enabling structured search. Despite steady progress, fully automated biomedical
EL still plateaus well below the precision required for curated resources: strong
systems on recent shared tasks report top-1 accuracy in the range of roughly
$0.7$~\cite{bionne,belb}. As a result, high-quality resource construction remains
a human-in-the-loop activity in which expert curators verify, correct, and
disambiguate system suggestions.

Three gaps make this curation harder than it needs to be. First,
\emph{short mentions and abbreviations} (``DM'', ``MI'', ``HTN'') are a systematic
weakness of dense retrievers: with aggressive sub-word tokenisation and short
maximum lengths, their embeddings carry little semantic signal, so the correct
concept is frequently absent from the candidate list the annotator sees. Second,
candidates are typically presented as \emph{flat text cards}---an identifier, a
label, a few aliases---which strips away the relational structure that makes
disambiguation easy for a human (is this ``lung'' an anatomical structure or a
disease?). Third, EL is usually performed \emph{one mention at a time}, so
decisions that ought to cohere across a document---two mentions of the same
concept, or two related concepts---are made independently and can silently
conflict.

We address these gaps with \emph{HELIX}, a configurable, open-source
expert-in-the-loop annotation tool. HELIX combines (i) hybrid dense$+$lexical
retrieval that recovers short-mention candidates a dense encoder misses;
(ii) a grounded large-language-model (LLM) re-ranker that picks the best
candidate with a rationale and confidence, constrained to the retrieved
identifiers; and (iii) a two-level, graph-aware review process backed by a Neo4j
knowledge graph. At the \emph{individual} level, the annotator sees each
mention's candidates together with their graph neighbourhood; at the
\emph{document} level, all accepted entities are drawn as one subgraph in which
isolated picks are flagged and can be renegotiated until the document is globally
coherent, turning independent per-mention choices into a human-driven collective
linking step.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \emph{hybrid retrieval} back end (SAPBERT$+$FAISS dense retrieval fused
        with a BM25/character-$n$-gram lexical index via reciprocal rank fusion)
        that targets the short-mention failure mode of dense EL
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:retrieval}).
  \item A \emph{grounded LLM re-ranker} (retrieval-augmented) that selects among
        retrieved candidates with rationale and confidence and cannot invent
        out-of-KB identifiers (Sec.~\ref{sec:llm}).
  \item A \emph{two-level graph-aware annotation workflow}: candidate-neighbourhood
        disambiguation and a document-level coherence-negotiation view that flags
        isolated picks for correction (Sec.~\ref{sec:graph}).
  \item An \emph{open, reproducible system}---multi-select annotation, provenance
        logging, containerised deployment, and an evaluation harness---together
        with an empirical study on a biomedical corpus (Secs.~\ref{sec:impl}--\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsubsection{Biomedical entity linking back ends.}
Neural biomedical EL is dominated by bi-encoder retrieval over synonym-rich
representations. BioSyn~\cite{biosyn} introduced synonym marginalisation, and
SAPBERT~\cite{sapbert} self-aligns biomedical entity representations against UMLS
synonymy, becoming the standard candidate generator. Lexical baselines such as
QuickUMLS~\cite{quickumls} and scispaCy~\cite{scispacy} remain competitive on
exact and near-exact matches and are fast and training-free. Benchmarks such as
BELB~\cite{belb} unify heterogeneous corpora and KBs, and the BioNNE-L shared
task~\cite{bionne} provides a multilingual evaluation setting. HELIX uses SAPBERT
retrieval as one strand but pairs it with an explicit lexical strand precisely
because dense encoders underperform on short mentions.

\subsubsection{Retrieval-and-rerank and LLMs for EL.}
The retrieve-and-rerank paradigm separates candidate generation from
disambiguation. Recent work applies LLMs to the reranking stage: retrieval-augmented
concept recognition~\cite{real}, constrained/contrastive decoding for biomedical
EL~\cite{llm4bioel}, generative re-ranking~\cite{belink}, alias-based retrieval
with LLM ranking for cross-lingual EL~\cite{bioelx}, and long-context,
document-consistent linking~\cite{longbel}. HELIX follows this paradigm but keeps
the human in the loop: the LLM produces a \emph{grounded} suggestion (restricted
to retrieved identifiers, with a rationale and confidence) that the annotator
confirms or overrides, rather than an autonomous prediction.

\subsubsection{Graph-based and collective disambiguation.}
A long line of work exploits KB structure for disambiguation, from topic-coherence
over semantic graphs~\cite{collective2020} and Personalized-PageRank collective
linking to entity-/relationship-embedding methods on knowledge
graphs~\cite{kgdisamb2021}. These systems infer coherence \emph{automatically};
HELIX instead \emph{surfaces} the graph neighbourhood to a human and makes the
document-level coherence check an interactive, auditable step---a
human-driven analogue of collective disambiguation.

\subsubsection{Annotation tools.}
General-purpose annotation platforms such as BRAT~\cite{brat} and
INCEpTION~\cite{inception} support entity and KB-linking annotation, and
INCEpTION adds machine assistance and active learning.
MedCATTrainer~\cite{medcattrainer} targets biomedical NER$+$L with active
learning. Evaluation and review tooling includes ELEVANT~\cite{elevant} for
fine-grained EL error analysis and AnNER~\cite{anner} for annotation/review
workflows in KG construction. Table~\ref{tab:tools} positions HELIX against these
tools: to our knowledge HELIX is the first annotation tool to combine hybrid
retrieval, a grounded LLM reranker, and a graph-neighbourhood/document-coherence
review surface in one workflow.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:system}

HELIX is a client--server application (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). A React front end
talks to a FastAPI service, which orchestrates three back ends: a knowledge-base
(KB) server exposing dense and lexical retrieval, an LLM re-ranker, and a Neo4j
graph. All components are configurable per annotation session; the tool is
domain-agnostic and works with any FAISS-indexable KB, with UMLS as our running
case.

\subsection{Hybrid Candidate Retrieval}
\label{sec:retrieval}

For a mention $m$, HELIX obtains two candidate lists. The \emph{dense} list comes
from SAPBERT~\cite{sapbert} embeddings indexed with FAISS~\cite{faiss} and scored
by cosine similarity. The \emph{lexical} list comes from an index built at
start-up from the same KB metadata, combining BM25~\cite{bm25} over alias
documents with a character-$n$-gram inverted index (bigrams and trigrams) that
matches abbreviations and misspellings the tokeniser would fragment. The two
lexical signals are merged with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)~\cite{rrf}:
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in R} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)},
\label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}
%
where $R$ is the set of ranked lists (BM25, $n$-gram) and $k$ is a smoothing
constant. The dense and lexical lists are then fused by identifier
(de-duplicating, keeping the higher score) into a single ranked candidate set
that HELIX presents to the annotator and passes to the reranker.

The lexical strand is the component that recovers short mentions: an exact or
near-exact alias match for ``DM'' surfaces \emph{Diabetes Mellitus} even when the
dense encoder ranks it far down the list. Sec.~\ref{sec:eval} quantifies this
effect by mention length.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Grounded LLM Re-ranking}
\label{sec:llm}

When enabled, an LLM re-ranks the fused candidate set (retrieval-augmented
generation): it receives the mention, its document context, and the top-$N$
candidates with labels, aliases, semantic types, and definitions, and returns a
single chosen identifier, a one-sentence rationale, and a calibrated confidence.
Crucially the choice is \emph{grounded}: the identifier is constrained to the
provided candidate set (enforced through a strict enumerated schema), so the LLM
cannot hallucinate an out-of-KB concept. Requests use a fixed, cached system
prompt and are memoised on disk keyed by the mention and candidate set, making
runs cheap and reproducible; any failure falls back silently to the retrieval
top-1 so the annotator is never blocked. The prompt and model are configurable
per session (Sec.~\ref{sec:eval} compares models).

\subsection{Graph-Aware Disambiguation}
\label{sec:graph}

HELIX connects to a Neo4j knowledge graph whose nodes are concepts
($\mathtt{(:Concept\ \{cui,\ name\})}$) and whose edges are typed relations
($\mathtt{[:RELATES\_TO\ \{rel\}]}$). Two views expose this structure.

\paragraph{Individual level.}
For a mention's candidate set $C$, HELIX queries how the candidates connect to one
another and to their one-hop neighbours (Listing~\ref{lst:cypher}), and renders a
compact node-link graph in a floating, resizable panel. Candidates that inhabit
different graph neighbourhoods are visibly distinct concepts, which makes
type-level disambiguation immediate.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Candidate-neighbourhood query (edges among candidates
plus capped one-hop neighbours).},label={lst:cypher},language=SQL]
MATCH (a:Concept)-[r:RELATES_TO]-(b:Concept)
WHERE a.cui IN $ids AND b.cui IN $ids AND a.cui < b.cui
RETURN a.cui AS source, b.cui AS target, r.rel AS rel
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Document level.}
Once every mention in a document has been annotated, HELIX assembles the accepted
identifiers into one subgraph. Reciprocal edges are collapsed and each accepted
concept is assigned a degree; concepts with no edge to any other accepted concept
are flagged as \emph{isolated}. The reviewer inspects this subgraph, and for any
isolated pick can optionally overlay that mention's alternative candidates
(drawn on an outer ring in a distinct colour) to see which candidate connects to
the rest of the document. Selecting a better-connected candidate rewrites the
annotation and re-computes the coherence flags. This makes collective
disambiguation an interactive, auditable step rather than an opaque inference,
echoing coherence-based collective linking~\cite{collective2020,kgdisamb2021}
while keeping the human in control.

\subsection{Annotation Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

The annotator sees, for each mention, the source document with the mention
highlighted in context and a ranked, dual-view candidate list (dense and
lexical). Selections are \emph{multi-valued}: a mention may map to several correct
concepts, recorded pipe-joined (e.g.\ \texttt{C0011849|C0011860}). A configurable
\emph{auto-select} mechanism pre-selects the top candidate (the LLM's pick when
re-ranking is on, otherwise the highest-scoring candidate, with entity-type-aware
tie-breaking) and advances after an adjustable countdown, which the annotator can
interrupt. Every decision---accept, override, manual entry, NIL---is written to a
self-documenting output, enabling the acceptance/override analysis in
Sec.~\ref{sec:eval}. Figures~\ref{fig:ui}~and~\ref{fig:docreview} show the
annotation and document-review surfaces.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:impl}

HELIX is implemented as a React/TypeScript front end, a FastAPI (Python) API, and
a Flask KB server that loads a SAPBERT FAISS index and builds the lexical index at
start-up. The graph store is Neo4j; the LLM re-ranker uses a configurable
provider. Connection and schema settings live in version-controlled configuration
files, and the entire stack ships as containers (one service per container: KB,
API, web, Neo4j) so the system can be reproduced with a single command; a local
launcher is also provided. A separate utility converts UMLS \texttt{MRREL} and
\texttt{MRCONSO} into Neo4j bulk-import CSVs, attaching English concept names to
every node. The source, configuration schema, and evaluation harness are
released.\footnote{\todo{repository URL (anonymised for review if required).}}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

HELIX is deliberately positioned as \emph{assistive}: the LLM and graph components
inform a human decision rather than replace it, and the document-level step is a
human-driven analogue of collective disambiguation rather than automatic
inference. This design keeps annotations auditable---every suggestion, override,
and renegotiation is logged---which matters for downstream KG construction.

Several limitations apply. Our study uses a single annotator, so we report
suggestion-acceptance and coherence-reduction rather than inter-annotator
agreement; a multi-annotator study is future work. The graph view depends on the
richness of the underlying KB relations---on a graph dominated by cross-vocabulary
mapping edges, coherence is weaker than on one with hierarchical relations, though
HELIX allows relation-type filtering to focus the view. Finally, LLM re-ranking
adds cost and latency and depends on an external service; HELIX mitigates this
with prompt caching, on-disk memoisation, and graceful fallback, and remains fully
functional with re-ranking disabled.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented HELIX, an expert-in-the-loop biomedical entity-linking annotation
tool that combines hybrid dense$+$lexical retrieval, grounded LLM re-ranking, and
a two-level graph-aware review process---candidate-neighbourhood disambiguation
and a document-level coherence-negotiation view. HELIX targets three concrete
pain points in EL curation: short-mention retrieval failures, flat candidate
presentation, and incoherent per-mention decisions. We release the system, its
configuration, and an evaluation harness, and report an empirical study on a
biomedical corpus. \todo{one-sentence headline result once numbers are final.}
